# Coaching for focus and retention

Not a medical document and not a diagnosis. Practical tutoring adjustments for
any student who finds it hard to stay focused and to retain what was learnt.

The central idea: **Their attention is a budget, not a character trait.** Every
sentence you write, every instruction, every decision you ask the learner to make
spends some of it. Spend it on science.

---

## Session shape — 25 minutes

Long sessions do not produce more learning from a student who cannot sustain
focus; they produce the same learning plus a bad association with the subject.
Two 25-minute sessions on different days beat one 50-minute session by a wide
margin, because the gap between them is where consolidation happens.

```
0:00  Warm restart      3 questions from previous sessions, no notes, out loud
0:03  Name the target   one sentence, and the finish time
0:05  Teach             ONE idea, with a physical model
0:09  Test it           2-3 questions on that idea, immediate feedback
0:14  Teach             second idea (maximum for one session)
0:18  Test it           mixed: new idea + one older sub-skill
0:23  Exit ticket       3 questions, marked on the spot
0:25  Stop              log the results, say what tomorrow is
```

**Stop at 25 minutes even mid-flow.** Especially mid-flow. Ending while it is
still going well is what makes the learner willing to start tomorrow. Overrunning
once costs you three future sessions.

If the learner is having a bad focus day, drop to 10 minutes and do only the warm
restart plus one idea. A completed 10-minute session is infinitely better
than an abandoned 25-minute one.

## The techniques that actually work

Ranked by evidence strength for durable retention. The first two do most of
the work.

### 1. Retrieval practice — testing as learning, not as measurement
Pulling an answer *out* of memory strengthens it far more than putting it in
again by re-reading. This is why every teaching block here is immediately
followed by a test, and why sessions open with unprompted recall.

Practically: never let the learner look at the notes while answering. Close the book,
answer, then check. The struggle to retrieve *is* the mechanism — a wrong
answer followed by correction beats a right answer read off the page.

### 2. Spaced repetition — revisit just before forgetting
The learner record schedules reviews at 1, 3, 7, 16 and 35 days, resetting
to day 1 on a failure. For a student who forgets, this is the single most
important piece of infrastructure in the skill. Trust the schedule over the
feeling that something is "already known".

### 3. Interleaving — mix topics within a session
Blocked practice (ten circuit questions in a row) feels productive and
produces weak retention, because after question three the learner is pattern-matching
rather than thinking. Mixed practice feels harder and retains much better.
Always include one question from an older sub-skill in every block.

### 4. Dual coding — words plus a picture, same idea
Say "current is the same everywhere in series" *and* show the circuit *and*
have the learner trace it with a finger. Three encodings, three retrieval routes.
This is a large part of why the diagrams in this skill must be real figures
rather than text descriptions.

### 5. Elaborative interrogation — one "why" after every right answer
"Correct — why?" Right answers for wrong reasons look identical to right
answers for right reasons until the exam. One extra question exposes it.

### 6. Concrete before abstract
Bicycle chain before "current is conserved". Expressway lanes before
"effective resistance decreases". The abstract statement is what the learner writes in
the exam; the concrete model is what lets the learner reconstruct it when the learner has
forgotten the sentence.

### 7. Teach-back
"Explain that to me as if I'm in Sec 1." Fluent explanation is the only
honest test of understanding, and it takes thirty seconds.

### 8. Mnemonics — for arbitrary facts only
Use them where there is no logic to hang the fact on, not as a substitute for
understanding:
- **A**mmeter = **A**long the path (series). Voltmeter = across.
- Current flows **through**, potential difference is **across**.
- Three effects, three homes: Chemical → liquids, Heating → kettle and fuse,
  Magnetic → electromagnet and motor.
- Fuse rating: **slightly** higher. Not equal, not much higher.
- Black and dull = best absorber **and** best emitter.
- Expansion changes the **spacing**, not the size or number of particles.
- Arrows in a food chain point **towards the eater**.

## Reducing the attention cost

**One question on screen at a time.** Not a list of five. Ask, wait, respond,
next. A list invites skimming, and skimming is where the learner disappears.

**Short paragraphs.** If a reply is more than about six lines without a
question in it, cut it.

**No choices you can make for the learner.** "Do you want to do circuits or heat
today?" spends attention on a decision that the learner record has already answered.
Say "Today is circuits" and start.

**Externalise everything.** The learner record, not their memory, holds what the learner
knows. The timer, not their sense of time, holds the finish line. The mark
scheme, not their judgement, holds whether an answer scored. Removing these
loads frees capacity for thinking.

**Make progress visible.** Show the learner the mastery bars from the learner record
periodically. A sub-skill moving from 35% to 78% is motivating in a way that
"you're improving" is not. Progress the learner can see is the main renewable source
of willingness.

**Allow movement and speech.** Answering out loud, pacing, tracing circuits
with a finger — all of it helps and none of it is a discipline problem.

## Feedback that works

**Be specific, and be about the work.**

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "Good job!" | "That's the keyword the marker wants — *effective resistance*." |
| "Wrong." | "Right idea, wrong word. You said 'voltage through'. Try again with 'across'." |
| "You need to work harder." | "That's a keyword error, not a concept error. You knew it — you just didn't write it the way the marker needs." |
| "You always forget this." | "This one's due for review today, that's normal. Second time round it sticks." |

**Distinguish the error type out loud.** Telling the learner "you understood that, you
just phrased it wrong" is genuinely different information from "you don't
understand this", and a student with a history of struggling usually assumes
the worse one by default. Naming the difference is one of the more useful
things this whole skill does.

**Never fake a mark.** If an answer scores 1 out of 3, say 1 out of 3, then
immediately show what the other two marks needed and have the learner rewrite it.
Inflating scores destroys the only signal the learner record runs on, and the learner will
discover the truth at the worst possible moment.

**Normalise forgetting.** The learner will forget things the learner knew last week. That is
what the spacing schedule is for — it is designed around forgetting, not
broken by it. Say so, plainly, whenever it happens.

## Handling the bad day

Signs: one-word answers, "I don't know" to things the learner answered yesterday,
asking how much longer.

Response — do not push through:
1. Drop to a 10-minute session. Say so out loud.
2. Recall only, nothing new. Questions the learner can get right.
3. Finish on something the learner definitely knows.
4. Log honestly. A bad day is data too — if the learner record later shows a cluster
   of `blank` errors on particular days, that is worth knowing.

Pushing a distracted student through new material teaches the learner that the
subject is unpleasant, which costs more than the twenty minutes saved.

---

## For the parent

- Two 25-minute sessions a week beats one long one, reliably.
- Ask the learner to *teach you* one thing from the session. If the learner can't, it hasn't
  landed yet — that is information, not failure.
- The parent view is readable without any science knowledge. The mastery
  bars and the "why marks are being lost" table tell you whether the problem
  is understanding or exam technique, which are fixed in completely
  different ways.
- A `keyword`-heavy profile is good news: it means the learner understands more than
  their grades show, and technique is the fastest thing to fix.
