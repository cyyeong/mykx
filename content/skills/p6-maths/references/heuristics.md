# Problem-solving heuristics: which method?

Read this when a word problem appears and you must decide how to teach or check it.
The most-missed section is **Before-and-After** — read it in full the first time.

Every heuristic below follows the same teaching shape:
**Spot it** (the words that give it away) → **Set it up** (the picture/table) → **Do it** (steps) → **Check it** (a 5-second sanity check).

---

## 0. RHMC — the four-step habit for every problem sum

**R**ead twice · **H**ighlight numbers and comparison words · **M**ethod (name it out loud) · **C**heck (logic + units).
Memory hook: *Real Heroes Make Champions.*

for any learner, naming the method aloud before writing anything is the single most protective habit:
"This is a *Total Unchanged* question." If the learner can name it, the steps come back.

---

## 1. Units & Parts (the model-drawing engine) — 22 marks per paper

The idea behind Fractions, Ratio and Percentage word problems is the same: **turn every comparison into equal-sized boxes.**

| The question says… | Write… |
|---|---|
| A is $\frac{3}{4}$ of B | A → 3 units, B → 4 units |
| A : B = 2 : 5 | A → 2 units, B → 5 units |
| A is 40% of B | 40% = $\frac{2}{5}$ → A 2 units, B 5 units |
| A is 3 times B | B → 1 unit, A → 3 units |
| A is 20% more than B | B → 5 units, A → 6 units |
| $\frac{2}{3}$ of A = $\frac{1}{2}$ of B | A → 3 units gives 2; B → 4 units gives 2 (make the equal parts the SAME number of units) |

**Golden rule:** *Units* come from one comparison; *parts* from another. When two comparisons describe the same thing, make the shared quantity the same number of units (find the LCM).

Then: find what **1 unit** is worth, then answer the question that was actually asked.

**Check:** Does 1 unit come out as a whole number? (Almost always in PSLE.) If it's a messy decimal, a set-up step is wrong.

## 2. Before-and-After — 4 types

These are the questions where a ratio/fraction/percentage is given **before** something happens, and a different one **after**. The whole game is answering one question first:

> **"What stayed the same?"**

Write the answer in words before any numbers. Then use the Before / Change / After table.

```
            |  Quantity 1  |  Quantity 2  |  Total or Difference
  Before    |              |              |
  Change    |              |              |
  After     |              |              |
```

Put **units** in the Before row and **parts** in the After row. Then make the unchanged column the same number (LCM) so units and parts become comparable.

### Type 1 — Difference Unchanged
**Spot it:** ages ("in 5 years' time…"), or *both* people gain the same amount, or *both* spend/lose the same amount ("each bought a $45 bag", "gave away an equal number").
**Why:** if both go up by the same amount, the gap between them doesn't move. Hook: *the age gap between you and Mum never changes.*

Example: Ravi and Siti had stickers in the ratio 5 : 3. Each of them gave away 12 stickers. The ratio became 3 : 1. How many stickers did Ravi have at first?

```
            |  Ravi  |  Siti  |  Difference
  Before    |  5u    |  3u    |  2u
  Change    |  −12   |  −12   |  0
  After     |  3p    |  1p    |  2p
```
The difference is 2 units before and 2 parts after, so 1 unit = 1 part. 5u − 12 = 3u → 2u = 12 → 1u = 6. Ravi at first: 5u = **30 stickers**. (Check: 30 − 12 = 18, 18 − 12 = 6, 18 : 6 = 3 : 1 ✓)

### Type 2 — Total Unchanged
**Spot it:** *internal transfer* — one person gives to the other ("Judy gave some of their stickers to Lindy"); or "some moved from box A to box B".
**Why:** what one loses the other gains; the total is untouched.

Example: Ali had $\frac{3}{5}$ as many marbles as Ben. After Ben gave Ali 14 marbles, they had the same number. How many marbles did Ben have at first?
```
            |  Ali  |  Ben  |  Total
  Before    |  3u   |  5u   |  8u
  After     |  1p   |  1p   |  2p    ×4 →  4u | 4u | 8u
```
Ben: 5u → 4u, so he gave 1u = 14. Ben at first: 5 × 14 = **70 marbles**.

### Type 3 — One Quantity Unchanged
**Spot it:** only one item is bought/eaten/sold/added; the other is never mentioned in the change. ("Mei ate $\frac{2}{3}$ of Mei's own sweets" — Tom's never change.) Also *external* additions: "some yellow beads were added" → the green ones are unchanged.
**Why:** whatever isn't touched is the anchor. Make its units equal in both rows.

Example: The ratio of red beads to blue beads in a box was 4 : 7. Some red beads were added and the ratio became 3 : 4. There are 84 blue beads. How many red beads were added?
Blue unchanged → make it the same number of units: 4 : 7 = 16 : 28 and 3 : 4 = 21 : 28. 28 units = 84 → 1 unit = 3. Red went from 16 to 21 units: 5 units = **15 red beads** added.

### Type 4 — Both Quantities Change (units and parts)
**Spot it:** two different amounts change ("bought 30 blue and 3 red"), or two different fractions are removed ("$\frac{1}{2}$ of the green and $\frac{3}{4}$ of the blue were taken out").
**Why:** nothing is fixed, so you write two expressions for the *after* and equate. This is the hardest type; often solvable with a bar model where the "after" parts are lined up.

Example: At first, $\frac{3}{4}$ of the pens in a box were blue and the rest were red. After 20 blue pens and 16 red pens were added, $\frac{2}{3}$ of the pens were blue. How many blue pens were there at first?
Before: blue 3u, red 1u. After: blue 2p, red 1p.
Red: 1u + 16 = 1p, so 2p = 2u + 32. Blue: 3u + 20 = 2p = 2u + 32 → 1u = 12 → blue at first = 3 × 12 = **36**.

When a Type-4 gets algebraic, it's fine for a P6 to use a letter: let 1 unit = *u*.

### The decision card (a one-liner to learn by heart)
> **Give/receive between the two → TOTAL same. Both change by the same number → DIFFERENCE same. Only one is touched → THAT ONE same. Otherwise → write two "after" expressions.**

### Common errors to watch for
- Using "before" units directly with "after" parts without equalising the unchanged column.
- Forgetting that "ate $\frac{2}{3}$ of their sweets" means $\frac{1}{3}$ *left*.
- In transfers, forgetting the gap swings by **twice** the amount given (giver −26, receiver +26 → gap changes by 52).
- Answering the "after" quantity when the question asked "at first".

## 3. Repeated Identity (three-quantity ratios) — "long ratio"
**Spot it:** two separate ratios share one name: A : B = 3 : 2 and B : C = 5 : 6.
**Set it up:** the shared quantity must have the same number → LCM of its two values.
A : B = 3 : 2 → ×5 → 15 : 10; B : C = 5 : 6 → ×2 → 10 : 12 ⇒ A : B : C = 15 : 10 : 12.
**Then:** use the difference/total the question gives to find 1 unit.

## 4. Remainder concept
**Spot it:** "of the remainder / of the rest / of what was left".
**Set it up:** a branching bar: the whole → first cut → the remainder becomes a *new whole* for the next fraction.
Keep going until the final leftover; express everything as a fraction of the ORIGINAL by multiplying: $\frac{3}{5}$ of the remainder when the remainder is $\frac{2}{3}$ of the whole = $\frac{3}{5}×\frac{2}{3} = \frac{2}{5}$ of the whole.
## 5. Grouping / Gap & Difference
**Spot it:** two things sold in bundles ("3 for $4.90, 4 for $5.90"), equal numbers bought, a money difference; or two people with the same total count but different sub-counts.
**Set it up:** find a group size that works for both bundles (LCM of 3 and 4 = 12 items), compute the cost difference *per group*, then divide the total difference by it.
## 6. Assumption (supposition) method
**Spot it:** two types of items, total count known, total value known, per-item values differ (tickets, coins, hardcover/paperback, "$7 for each safe melon, −$2 for each damaged one").
**Do it:** assume ALL are type A → compute value → compare with actual → each swap to type B changes the value by (A − B) → number of B = difference ÷ swap size.
## 7. Working backwards
**Spot it:** the *end* amount is known and steps are described forward ("after a 20% discount and a further $4 off per ticket, 5 tickets cost $60").
**Do it:** reverse each step from the end, in reverse order, using the inverse operation. Draw a flow: start → ×0.8 → −4 → $12 per ticket. Reverse: 12 + 4 = 16; 16 ÷ 0.8 = **$20**.

## 8. Equal concept / equal stage
**Spot it:** two totals are equal, or the same quantity is re-split ("6 bottles broke, marbles shared among the remaining 30, each got 9 more").
**Do it:** 36 × m = 30 × (m + 9) → 6m = 270 → m = **45**. Or by units: total is fixed, so 36 units = 30 units + 270.

## 9. Substitution
**Spot it:** one item is expressed in terms of another ("3 storybooks cost the same as 2 water bottles"; "5 large boxes hold the same as 6 small boxes").
**Do it:** replace everything with ONE item type, solve, then convert back.

## 10. Guess & check / systematic listing
Use as a last resort or for "greatest/least number" questions with divisibility (219 apples into boxes of 15 and 6: 14 large leaves 9 — not a multiple of 6; 13 large leaves 24 = 4 small → **13**). Always list in a table and stop when the remainder works.

## 11. Number & figure patterns
Find (a) the repeating block or (b) the constant step. Write "Figure n → formula" explicitly:
- Repeating block (2 2 0 2 0 → block sum 6, length 5): 62 ÷ 5 = 12 blocks r 2 → 12 × 6 + (2 + 2) = **76**.
- Growing pattern: tiles 4, 7, 10, 13 → step 3 → Figure n = 3n + 1 → Figure 22 = **67**. Perimeter 10, 16, 22 → 6n + 4 = 100 → n = **16**.
Trap: check whether Figure 1 fits the formula (3 × 1 + 1 = 4 ✓).

## 12. Average
Average × number = total. Always go to TOTAL first. Combined average of two groups = combined total ÷ combined count (never the average of the averages). "Avg of 6 numbers is 35; first 4 sum to 188 → last 2 sum to 22 → avg 11."

## 13. Rate / charges tables ("or part thereof")
Duration → subtract the "first block" → divide the rest by the block size → **round UP** ("or part thereof") → multiply → add first-block charge. Backwards version: subtract the block charges from the total to find the first-block price.
## 14. Percentage change
% change = change ÷ **original** × 100%. Successive changes are multiplicative (×1.5 then ×0.5 = ×0.75 → 25% lower). Discount: final = (100 − d)% of original; GST/interest: final = (100 + r)%.
"Find the original" ⇒ divide by the percentage that the final represents (72°C is 75% → 72 ÷ 0.75 = 96).

## 15. Logic check
Before writing "Ans", ask: *Is a bookshelf 17 m tall? Is a coin 4 kg? Can half a child exist?* A ridiculous answer means a set-up error — go back to the model, not the arithmetic.
