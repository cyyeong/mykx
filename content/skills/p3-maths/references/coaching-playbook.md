# Coaching playbook: short, visual, game-like sessions that stick

Not a diagnosis. Practical adjustments for Primary 3 pupils who drift unless
the work is interesting, and who need things to stick.
The ideas below are the ones with the strongest evidence for retention
(retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, dual coding, immediate feedback)
dressed in a way a 9-year-old will put up with.

The governing idea: **attention is a budget**. Every sentence you write and
every decision you ask the learner to make spends some of it. Spend it on maths.

---

## 1. Session shape — 20 minutes, hard stop

Two 20-minute sessions on different days beat one 40-minute session, because
the gap is where memory consolidates. On a bad focus day, do 10 minutes:
warm restart plus one idea. A finished 10-minute session beats an abandoned
20-minute one every time.

```
0:00  Warm restart   3 quick questions from earlier sessions, no notes,
                     said out loud. Score them: "3 for 3 — that's a streak."
0:03  Name the target  "Today: comparison models with an extra. Fifteen
                     minutes, then you're done." Say the finish time.
0:04  Show           ONE idea with a picture (bar model, time line, grid).
0:08  Do             3 questions on it, immediate feedback after each one.
0:14  Mix            2 questions: today's idea + one older sub-skill.
0:17  Exit ticket    3 short questions, marked on the spot.
0:20  Stop. Log it. Say what tomorrow is.
```

Stop at 20 even mid-flow. *Especially* mid-flow — ending while it is still
going well is what makes the learner willing to start tomorrow.

---

## 2. Making it interesting enough to hold the learner

These are not decorations; for many children they are the delivery mechanism.

- **Beat-the-clock, not beat-the-teacher.** "Can you do these 4 table facts in
  40 seconds?" A visible timer turns a drill into a game and gives a clear
  end. Always set the time so the learner wins about 70% of the time.
- **Streaks and levels.** Keep a running streak count out loud. Three in a
  row "levels up" the question (bigger numbers, one more step). A miss resets
  the streak but not the level — losing progress feels bad; losing a streak
  feels like a game.
- **The learner sets the question for you.** Once per session: "Give me a comparison
  problem about football / a favourite game / whatever the learner likes, and I'll solve it."
  Writing a question is retrieval practice in disguise, and the learner will check your
  answer ferociously.
- **Contexts the learner cares about.** Swap "Mr Tan bought flour" for their own world —
  bus fares, game tokens, football scores, bubble tea. Keep the maths
  identical.
- **Error hunting.** Show a worked solution with one planted mistake ("Find
  the mistake — it's worth 2 points"). Finding errors is more engaging than
  producing answers and trains the NTUC check.
- **Choice.** "Which first: the time line question or the money one?" Choice
  costs nothing and buys engagement.
- **Movement break at 10 minutes.** Stand, 10 star jumps, back. Not a reward
  — a reset.
- **Drawing is doing.** Bar models, time lines and grids are drawings; a child
  who hates writing will often happily draw. Lead with the picture.

## 3. The techniques that make it stick

### Retrieval practice (the big one)
Pulling an answer *out* strengthens memory far more than putting it in again.
Never let the learner look at a worked example while answering. Close it, answer,
then check. A wrong answer followed by correction beats a right answer copied
off the page.

### Spaced review
Leitner boxes at 1, 2, 4, 7, 14 days (compressed for the run-in to the exam).
A sub-skill that fails goes back to day 1. Trust the schedule over the feeling
that something is "done".

### Interleaving
Never ten questions on one sub-skill. After the three "learn it" questions,
every set mixes topics. It feels harder; it retains better; and the real
paper is interleaved.

### Teach-back
"Explain that to me as if I'm your little cousin." If the learner can't say why Mary's
bar is longer, the learner doesn't have it yet, and you both find out in 30 seconds
instead of in the exam.

### Immediate, specific feedback
Right: "Yes — and *why* was Mary's bar longer?" (ask why once).
Wrong: name the error type out loud in kid language — "That's a *direction*
mistake, not a maths mistake. Fewer than Mary means Mary has more." Then the learner
redoes that one question, not three new ones.

### Worked example → faded example → their own
First a complete worked solution (the learner reads it aloud). Second, the same shape
with the last line missing. Third, blank. One archetype, three questions.

## 4. Memory hooks for P3 maths (use these exact ones so they stay consistent)

**Tables 6–9**
- 9s finger trick: hold up 10 fingers, fold the n-th finger; tens on the left,
  ones on the right. 9 × 7 → fold finger 7 → 6 | 3 → 63.
- 9s digit check: the digits of every 9-times answer add to 9 (63 → 6 + 3).
- "5, 6, 7, 8" → 56 = 7 × 8. Say it as a rhythm.
- 6 × even: the answer ends in the same digit (6 × 4 = 24, 6 × 8 = 48).
- 8s double-double-double: 8 × 7 = double 7 (14), double (28), double (56).
- Nearly-there facts: 7 × 6 = 7 × 5 + 7 = 35 + 7; 8 × 9 = 8 × 10 − 8.
- Daily 2-minute "flash" on only the 6 facts the learner misses most (the learner record shows
  which). Not the whole table.

**Fewer / more**
- "Fewer than *Mary*" → Mary's name is next to *fewer*, so Mary is the one who
  has **more**. Circle the name after the word.

**Perimeter vs area**
- Peri-meter = "walk around the *rim*". Fence, cm.
- Area = "carpet". Length × breadth, cm² (the little 2 is the carpet's two
  directions).

**Acute / obtuse**
- Acute is "a cute little angle" (small). Obtuse is "oh-big-tuse" (big, fat).
  Right angle = the corner of a page.

**Parallel / perpendicular**
- Para**ll**el has two l's standing side by side and never touching.
- Perpendicular makes a **T** or an **L** (the corner of a page fits in).

**Units**
- km → m: "**kilo** means a thousand" (a kilogram is a thousand grams, a
  kilometre is a thousand metres). 1 ℓ = 1000 ml the same way.
- 1 m = 100 cm: "a **cent**ury is 100 years".
- Convert *before* you calculate: write both numbers in the small unit first.

**Time**
- Time line, always. Jump to the next o'clock first.
- 24-hour: afternoon hours "+12" (2 p.m. → 14 00); before noon stays the same.
- 1 h = 60 min, not 100 — "clock numbers, not money numbers".

**Fractions**
- Equivalent: "whatever you do to the top, do to the bottom".
- Simplest form: "can I cut both with the same knife?" (divide both by 2, 3…)
- Comparing: "is it more or less than half?" as the first test.

**Model method**
- Longer bar first, line up on the left, write "u" in every equal box,
  question mark where the answer lives.
- Units are "u" — not x. "How many u's? What is all of them worth? What is
  one u worth?" — the three questions that solve every units problem.

**Checking — NTUC**
- **N**umber **T**ransfer (did I copy the numbers right?) · **U**nits (on every
  line and in the answer) · **C**alculation (re-add once) — the learner draws three
  tick-boxes in the margin: NT · U · C.

## 5. Diagnosing where the break is

For every sub-skill, climb three rungs and stop at the first failure:

| rung | question | if the learner fails here |
|---|---|---|
| R1 Fact/skill | "What is 8 × 7?" · "How many cm in 1 m?" | the fact is missing — build the hook, 2-min daily flash |
| R2 Straight application | "Perimeter of a 7 cm by 4 cm rectangle?" | the procedure is missing — worked → faded → own |
| R3 Word problem | "A wire is bent into a 7 by 4 rectangle, then a square…" | the *translation* is missing — draw the model together |

Do not teach above a broken rung. A pupil drilled on wire problems while their
perimeter procedure is shaky learns to guess.

**Error types to name out loud** (and log): concept · model · method ·
arithmetic · units · misread · presentation · blank · slip. The two that
dominate in P3 are *method* (chose the wrong operation — a reading problem)
and *presentation* (right idea, no number sentence). Both are fixable in days,
not weeks.

---

## 6. Feedback wording that works

- Short. One sentence of feedback, then the next question.
- Name the error type, not the child. "That's a units slip" not "you're careless".
- Praise the *method* you want repeated: "You drew the longer bar first —
  that's exactly the move." Not "good job".
- When the learner is stuck, give the *first line only*, never the whole solution:
  "Start by writing what Mary has." Then wait.
- When the learner gets frustrated: drop one level, get one right, stop the session on
  that win. The exam is in days; morale is a resource.
- Never say "this is easy". If the learner fails it, that sentence becomes "I can't
  even do easy ones".

---

## 7. The last 10 days before the paper

With about a week to go, the priority order flips from "learn" to "secure":

1. Days 10–6: two 20-minute sessions a day if possible; priority order from `learner_summary()`.
2. Days 5–3: one full 50-mark paper from the bank under time, marked, every
   lost mark logged with an error type. Then only the top-5 gaps.
3. Days 2–1: no new material. Warm restarts, table flashes, time line and
   units drills, one Section C model problem a day. Sleep.
4. Exam morning: the four-box UnPAC/NTUC margin checklist and "longer bar
   first". Nothing else.
